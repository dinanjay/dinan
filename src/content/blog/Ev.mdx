Inside the Manufacturing of Cylindrical Lithium-Ion Batteries

Understanding how cylindrical battery cells are manufactured, why manufacturers prefer this design, and how AI is changing battery production.

Lithium-ion batteries are everywhere, from smartphones and power tools to electric vehicles and large-scale energy storage systems. But have you ever wondered how a cylindrical battery cell is actually manufactured?

During a lecture on cylindrical lithium-ion cell manufacturing, we explored the production process, cell design, thermal management, and the growing role of artificial intelligence in battery factories.

Let's take a closer look.

1. How Are Cylindrical Battery Cells Manufactured?

Manufacturing a cylindrical lithium-ion battery involves several highly controlled processes. Each stage contributes to the cell's performance, safety, and reliability.

Electrode preparation

The process starts with electrodes coated with a slurry on both sides. These electrodes form the anode and cathode of the battery.

A separator is also required to prevent direct electrical contact between the electrodes while allowing lithium ions to move between them.

Laser cutting and winding

Laser cutting prepares the electrode tabs, which act as current collection points.

The anode, cathode, and separator are then fed into a winding machine. They are rolled together to create a structure known as the jelly roll.

This is one of the main characteristics of cylindrical battery manufacturing.

Cell assembly

Once the jelly roll is ready, it is inserted into a cylindrical metal can.

The current collectors are connected using laser welding, followed by a forming process that creates the neck of the cell.

Baking and electrolyte filling

The assembled cells undergo baking to remove remaining moisture.

After drying, electrolyte is introduced into the cell. The opening is then sealed through a mechanical process called crimping.

The outer surface receives an oiling treatment to protect the stainless-steel can against oxidation and corrosion.

Formation and testing

The final stage is formation.

Each cell receives a controlled charging and discharging program. Manufacturers then test important electrical parameters, including:

- Open-circuit voltage (OCV)
- Internal resistance
- Electrical performance

Only cells that meet the required specifications proceed to the next stage.

2. Why Are Cylindrical Cells So Popular?

One major advantage of cylindrical cells is their manufacturing efficiency.

Creating a jelly roll is relatively fast compared with producing other cell formats. Their metal casing also provides good mechanical strength.

However, cylindrical cells have one disadvantage: they leave gaps when packed together in a battery module.

Prismatic cells use available space more efficiently, reaching approximately 99.3–99.5% space utilisation in the examples discussed during the lecture.

Cylindrical designs compensate for this through their manufacturing advantages, mechanical strength, and thermal management possibilities.

Specialised foam materials can also fill the gaps between cells in battery modules.

3. The Importance of Cell Dimensions

Battery dimensions have a significant impact on energy capacity, internal resistance, and thermal behaviour.

In cylindrical cells, the diameter is fixed for a particular cell format. This limits the available space for the electrode assembly.

Manufacturers can increase capacity through several approaches:

- Using thinner electrode layers to accommodate more electrode length.
- Increasing the solid content of the electrode slurry.
- Adjusting the thickness of the electrode coating.
- Optimising the electrode and current collector design.

However, every adjustment involves trade-offs.

For example, thinner current collector foils can allow more active electrode material, but they may also increase internal resistance.

The lecture highlighted the 46–48 mm diameter range as an important design sweet spot, balancing cell capacity, resistance, and thermal behaviour.

The development of cylindrical cells has become particularly important in the automotive industry.

BMW's sixth-generation battery technology, used in its Neue Klasse platform, introduces several improvements over the previous generation.

5. Artificial Intelligence in Battery Manufacturing

Modern battery factories are increasingly moving towards Industry 4.0.

One example is the use of AI-powered vision systems to inspect manufactured cells.

Cell dimensions must be controlled very precisely. According to the lecture, cell height can require accuracy of approximately 0.1 mm.

Traditional inspection methods can identify defects, but AI-based systems can go further.

They can:

- Detect manufacturing defects using machine vision.
- Identify patterns in production data.
- Estimate manufacturing losses.
- Predict potential production problems.
- Alert managers before a problem becomes critical.

This changes the role of factory operators as well. Instead of performing repetitive manual tasks, workers increasingly focus on monitoring, maintaining, and managing automated production systems.

6. Thermal Management and Battery Safety

Temperature is one of the most important factors affecting lithium-ion battery safety.

Battery Management Systems (BMS) monitor electrical parameters such as current and voltage, alongside temperature measurements at selected critical locations.

Cell temperatures can also be estimated using electrical parameters and thermal models.

If temperatures become unsafe, the BMS can intervene by stopping charging or activating the battery's cooling system.

The lecture highlighted the following temperature ranges:

- 65–70°C: Increased risk of dangerous reactions.
- Around 80°C: A critical temperature range.

Proper thermal management is therefore essential for maintaining battery performance and preventing thermal runaway.

7. The Role of Standardisation

An interesting point from the lecture was that the fundamental production structure of lithium-ion cells has remained standardised since approximately 1991.

The main production stages include:

1. Mixing
2. Coating
3. Pressing
4. Winding
5. Assembly
6. Formation

Although manufacturing technologies have improved considerably, these fundamental stages remain important.

Standardisation can sometimes feel restrictive, but it has helped manufacturers develop reliable, repeatable production processes and improve battery technology over time.

Conclusion

Cylindrical lithium-ion battery manufacturing is much more than simply rolling electrodes into a metal container.

It combines material science, mechanical engineering, electrical engineering, automation, and advanced quality control.

From the jelly roll manufacturing process to AI-powered inspection systems, every stage contributes to the final battery's performance and reliability.

As electric vehicles and energy storage systems continue to develop, improvements in battery manufacturing will remain just as important as advances in battery chemistry.

Understanding how these cells are manufactured gives us a better appreciation of the engineering behind modern energy storage.
